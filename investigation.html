Tulunad: The Jaffna of South India

There are lands conquered by armies. There are lands erased by maps. And there are lands slowly weakened by paperwork, resource extraction, and administrative silence. Tulunad belongs to the third category.

The "Biological Frontier" is marked by a century of what activists term "Biological and Institutional Genocide." Between 1980 and 2001, state-sanctioned chemical attrition via Endosulfan claimed an estimated 30,000 souls in the Tuluva heartland. This was not merely an agricultural accident but a systemic failure where thousands were denied essential medical facilities because they spoke only Tulu and were met with the indifferent silence of a monolingual bureaucracy.

Chapter II — The Netravati Frontier

The Ettinahole diversion debate is not merely an engineering issue. For many communities in Tulunad, the headwaters of the Netravati represent agriculture, ecology, memory, and intergenerational survival.

This is a battleground of conflicting data. While the government’s Detailed Project Report (DPR) insists on a divertible yield of 24 TMC, independent scientific assessments, such as IISc's Technical Report 91, estimate the actual runoff to be a meager 9.55 TMC. For the coastal fishing community, this is an existential crisis. Traditional elders calculate their seasonal catch by the volume of "palke"—the nutrient-rich organic deposits brought by the river. A 30% reduction in freshwater flow would destroy this delicate salinity balance, likely triggering a mass migration of fish species and turning independent Tuluva fishers into mere laborers on deep-sea trawlers.

Chapter III — The Jaffna Parallel

The parallel is rooted in a history of perceived marginalization that dates back to the first call for a separate Tulu Nadu in 1942 by Srinivas Updhyaya Paniyadi. Today, regional seers and activists describe the coastal districts as a "laboratory of experiments" for harmful state projects. Despite being the highest revenue-generating districts for the state, the region feels treated as a resource colony, where water rights are extracted to fuel the growth of distant capitals while local ecological concerns are sidelined.

Across South Asia, questions of language, land, and water have often shaped regional identity. The comparison to Jaffna here is editorial: not equivalence, but a reflection on how communities may interpret administrative exclusion, resource policy, and historical memory.

Chapter IV — The 60-Day Blackout

Sixty days have passed. The Action Taken Report remains unanswered. For citizens waiting on institutional response, silence itself becomes political.

The current 60-day silence revolves around the unanswered Action Taken Report (ATR) regarding the blackening of the Gurupura river—a vital Netravathi tributary—due to industrial effluents in the Baikampady area. While fish kills and "glaring" pollution control failures have been flagged by joint committees, institutional response has stalled. The National Green Tribunal (NGT) has recently demanded a fresh report within six weeks to address these failures, with a critical hearing scheduled for May 15, 2026.
